Star sapphires exhibit asterism, an optical phenomenon in which intersecting light bands form a multi-rayed star pattern across the gem's surface. Asterism is caused by reflection of light off microscopic, needle-like rutile inclusions aligned systematically along the crystallographic planes of the host corundum crystal.

In standard six-rayed star sapphires, rutile needles crystallize parallel to the three basal axes of the hexagonal corundum structure at 60-degree angles. When overhead light strikes these oriented needles, three bright light rays intersect at a central epicenter. In rare instances, twelve-rayed star sapphires occur when rutile needles co-exist with oriented hematite needles along two distinct crystallographic orientation systems.

To display a crisp, centered star, star sapphires must be cut as smooth, domed cabochons rather than faceted gems. The cabochon dome must be oriented precisely parallel to the c-axis of the rough crystal, with the epicenter located directly at the top center of the dome.

Star sapphires occur in blue, gray, pink, yellow, and black body colors. Value is determined by the clarity, brightness, straightness, and centralization of the star rays, combined with the saturation and transparency of the underlying body color. Top-quality star sapphires display sharp, continuous rays that glide smoothly across the cabochon surface as the light source shifts.
