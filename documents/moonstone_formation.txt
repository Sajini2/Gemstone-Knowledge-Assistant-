Moonstone belongs to the feldspar mineral group, which constitutes the most abundant mineral family in the Earth's crust. Specifically, gem-quality moonstone is an intergrowth of two alkali feldspar end-members: potassium-rich orthoclase (KAlSi3O8) and sodium-rich albite (NaAlSi3O8).

The formation of moonstone begins at high temperatures deep within igneous pegmatites or metamorphic magmatic bodies, where orthoclase and albite exist as a homogeneous solid solution. As the surrounding magma or hydrothermal system slowly cools over extended geological timeframes, the solid solution becomes thermodynamically unstable.

This temperature drop triggers a phase transition known as exsolution, wherein the sodium and potassium ions separate without breaking the overall silicate framework. Albite precipitates out as thin, microscopic lamellar plates embedded within the host orthoclase crystal matrix. The cooling rate dictates the physical scale of these exsolution lamellae: slow cooling yields well-ordered, alternating layers optimal for light interference, while rapid cooling prevents phase separation, yielding non-adularescent feldspar.

The chemical purity of the parent melt influences clarity. Iron impurities can impart brownish or reddish tints, whereas low-iron, volatile-rich granitic pegmatites provide the pristine chemical conditions required for water-clear, transparent moonstone formation.
