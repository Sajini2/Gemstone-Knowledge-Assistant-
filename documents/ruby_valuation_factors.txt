The valuation of natural rubies is determined by five primary criteria: color, clarity, carat weight, cut, and geographical origin. Among these, color is the dominant factor governing market value. The ideal ruby color, historically termed "Pigeon's Blood," exhibits a pure, vivid crimson hue with medium to medium-dark tone and high color saturation, accompanied by strong red fluorescence under ultraviolet light.

Clarity plays a significant role, though rubies are rarely eye-clean compared to diamonds. Eye-clean rubies above one carat command immense premiums. Fine rutile inclusions that do not obscure transparency are tolerated and often prized, as they diffuse light evenly across the gemstone facets. However, surface-reaching fractures, heavy structural clouds, or large dark mineral inclusions substantially reduce value.

Carat weight dramatically influences price per carat due to scarcity. High-quality natural rubies exceeding three carats are exponentially rarer than smaller stones, resulting in non-linear pricing escalations. Cut quality dictates optical brilliance and color distribution. Symmetrical proportions, appropriate crown height, and precise facet angles optimize light reflection while preventing light leakage through the pavilion.

Finally, verified origin heavily influences trade value. Marble-hosted rubies from traditional Burmese sources (such as Mogok) command top market prices over stones from secondary deposits, owing to historical prestige and vivid fluorescent properties. Independent lab certificates documenting origin and treatment status are indispensable for establishing accurate commercial valuations.
