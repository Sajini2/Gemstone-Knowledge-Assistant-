Rubies are the red gem variety of the aluminum oxide mineral corundum, achieving their vivid color through trace amounts of chromium replacing aluminum in the crystal lattice. The geological formation of ruby requires a rare combination of aluminum-rich, silica-deficient environments where chromium is accessible during high-grade regional metamorphism or magmatic processes.

The premier origin type is marble-hosted deposit formation. During collisional tectonics, such as the Himalayan orogeny, aluminum-rich limestones undergo intense heat and pressure, metamorphosing into marble. Hydrothermal fluids enriched with chromium permeate the marble beds, reacting with aluminum minerals to form high-clarity corundum crystals. Marble-hosted rubies, famous in regions like Mogok (Myanmar), Vietnam, and Afghanistan, are characteristically low in iron content. This low iron concentration allows chromium fluorescence to remain unquenched, producing an intense red glow under ambient sunlight.

In contrast, basalt-hosted ruby deposits form through magmatic events deep within the Earth's mantle. Rubies crystallize within mantle rocks and are subsequently transported to the surface by ascending alkali basalt magma currents. Found in places like Thailand, Cambodia, and Madagascar, basalt-hosted rubies incorporate elevated levels of iron into their crystal structure alongside chromium. High iron content quenches the natural red fluorescence, resulting in a darker, slightly brownish or purplish red hue. Understanding these geological mechanisms is essential for mapping deposit distributions and interpreting geochemical origin signatures in raw corundum deposits.
