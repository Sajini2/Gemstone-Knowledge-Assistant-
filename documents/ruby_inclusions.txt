Inclusions in rubies provide vital evidence regarding the gem's natural origin, geographic provenance, and thermal history. The most notable inclusion species found in high-grade natural corundum is rutile (titanium dioxide), which crystallizes as microscopic needle-like formations known in gemology as "silk." These needles form along the three crystallographic axes of the corundum crystal at 60-degree and 120-degree angles. When present in dense, uninterrupted intersecting networks, rutile silk causes light scattering, which imparts a soft, velvety luster characteristic of premium untreated rubies.

Beyond rutile silk, rubies frequently host solid mineral inclusions such as calcite, apatite, zircon, and boehmite. Zircon crystals are often surrounded by stress halos or tension fractures resulting from differential thermal expansion during geological metamorphism. Liquid-filled healed fractures, commonly referred to as "fingerprint inclusions," present net-like patterns created when mineral-rich fluid trapped inside microscopic cracks recrystallizes over geological timescales.

Gemological laboratories evaluate these inclusions to identify treatments. Intact, crisp rutile needles confirm that a ruby has not been subjected to high-temperature heat treatment. Conversely, melted silk, partially dissolved mineral grains, or ruptured halo fractures indicate thermal enhancement above 1,500 degrees Celsius. Inclusions also assist in geographic origin determination; for example, marble-hosted Burmese rubies typically feature dense silk and nested calcite rhombohedrons, whereas basalt-hosted rubies from Thailand feature minimal silk and prominent iron-rich disk fractures.
