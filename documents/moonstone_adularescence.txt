Adularescence is the characteristic optical phenomenon observed in fine moonstones, manifesting as a soft, billowy sheen or floating light luminescence beneath the gem's surface. This effect is named after Mount Adula in Switzerland, a historic source of high-quality feldspar moonstones.

The physical origin of adularescence lies in microscopic structural unmixing during the cooling history of potassium-rich alkali feldspar. As high-temperature sodium-potassium feldspar cools slowly, orthoclase (KAlSi3O8) and albite (NaAlSi3O8) separate into alternating, microscopic layers or exsolution lamellae. When ambient light strikes these sub-microscopic layers—which range from 50 to 100 nanometers in thickness—the light rays undergo Rayleigh scattering and interference.

The wavelength of scattered light depends on the thickness of the alternating albite lamellae. Extremely thin layers (under 100 nanometers) preferentially scatter shorter blue wavelengths of light, producing a vivid, floating blue schiller across the gemstone. Thicker lamellae produce white or yellowish adularescent sheens.

To showcase adularescence to maximum advantage, lapidaries must cut moonstones into cabochons with high domes, orienting the base parallel to the feldspar cleavage planes (001). Correct orientation ensures that the diffuse glow glides smoothly across the top of the cabochon as the stone is tilted relative to light sources.
